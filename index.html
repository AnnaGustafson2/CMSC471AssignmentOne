A brief introduction to your dataset, background and your chosen variable(s) or subset

We chose to create a visualization using the Daily Weather in the U.S., 2017 dataset, which contains daily weather measurements collected across the United States throughout 2017. The data comes from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) Global Historical Climatology Network. For our visualization, we focused on four key weather variables: average temperature, maximum temperature, minimum temperature, and precipitation. We also incorporated date and geographic location (latitude and longitude) to allow users to explore how weather conditions vary across different locations and throughout the year.

Rationale for your design decisions. List your implemented interactions. Explain: How did you choose your particular visual encodings and interaction techniques? What alternatives did you consider and how did you arrive at your ultimate choices?

To accurately display the locations of the weather records, we decided to use a map of the United States with colored dots representing temperature and precipitation. This allows users to easily identify weather patterns and trends across different locations.

Alternatives that we considered included a bar chart to visualize the precipitation and temperature over time. However, we thought that having each record on a map would be more useful when trying to compare trends throughout the entire US. We also initially only had precipitation and average temperature as the variables, but added more in order to give the user more options to explore.

For temperature, warmer colors represent higher temperatures, while cooler colors represent lower temperatures. We also added interactive tooltips that allow users to view more detailed weather information for a specific location.

For selecting weather variables, we implemented a dropdown menu. We chose this because it provides an intuitive way for users to view the available options, select a variable, and clearly see which variable is currently being displayed.

For the other controls, we implemented sliders. The date slider allows users to move chronologically through the year and observe how weather patterns change over time. We also included a date display that shows the exact date currently being visualized on the map.

Similarly, the latitude and longitude sliders allow users to adjust the range of coordinates displayed. This gives users the ability to focus on specific regions rather than viewing the entire country. For example, a user might want to examine temperature trends along the East Coast instead of across the entire United States.

Finally, we added a color legend at the bottom of the visualization to help users understand what each color represents. This makes it easier to interpret the data and compare weather conditions across different locations.

References to data resources. Be sure to list the data source you used. If your work adapts or builds on existing visualization examples, please cite those as well.

In our visualization, we utilized the TopoJSON Atlas for fast rendering of geographic states as well as latitude and longitude boundaries in the visualization (https://observablehq.com/@d3/u-s-map) Additionally, we used D3.js for all data loading, scales, and rendering as well as the D3.js slider utilized in the lab (https://observablehq.com/@mbostock/hello-d3-simple-slider). The data comes from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) Global Historical Climatology Network (Daily Weather in the US, 2017).

An overview of your development process. Describe how the work was split among the team members. Include a commentary on the development process, including answers to the following questions: Roughly how much time did you spend developing your application (in people-hours)? What aspects took the most time?

During the design process to determine the exact attributes we wanted to display in the project, Anna drew rough wireframes and proposed to display average temperature and precipitation over geographical regions with a date slider. Mahita narrowed the scope and created a Google Slide to illustrate how points would be displayed on a map as well as developed the latitude and longitude sliders. Finally, Kyra made a polished Figma and developed straightforward UX designs including color scales and legends. Each individual step took roughly two hours to complete.

Next we divided the work into sections for the development of the web page. Anna created the web page shell, loaded the data, and connected the variable dropdown and the date slider to the data points so that the data appeared and updated based on the user interaction. Mahita integrated the TopoJSON Atlas in order to have the points displayed upon a map of the United States, added longitude and latitude sliders, and created tooltips to explain additional information where the user hovers over data points. Kyra crafted the UX design to be intuitive and clean, fixed all labeling and inconsistency issues, and added legends. Each person's part took around three hours to complete.

Finally, Kyra recorded the demo video and everyone contributed to the writeup. This took an additional 1.5 hours a person to complete. In all it took around 20 people hours to make this visualization and the coding and debugging portion took the longest.
